# How I Work

Most technical writing portfolios show the output. This page is about the process that gets there.

---

## Gathering Information

I start with what already exists: Jira tickets, wiki pages, release notes, design docs. By the time I sit down with an engineer, I've done enough reading to ask specific questions instead of general ones. "What happens when the user skips the prerequisites?" gets better answers than "can you walk me through this feature?"

The interview isn't an interrogation — it's a way to fill the gaps I couldn't fill from reading. I take notes on what surprises me, because if something surprises me, it'll probably surprise the user too.

After the interview I write a first draft and send it for review with both the SME and a peer. Two readers catch different things. The engineer spots technical errors. The peer spots places where the logic doesn't land for someone without that context.

---

## When a SME Says "It's Obvious"

This happens more than you'd think. My answer is always the same: I'm not writing this for you.

The SME built the feature. Of course it's obvious to them. My job is to advocate for the user who hasn't, who might be coming from a different product, a different industry, or a different level of technical experience. When I push back on "it's obvious," I'm not questioning the engineer's work — I'm doing mine.

---

## When Engineers Rewrite My Prose

Sometimes an engineer makes changes in review and they're good. I accept them without argument. They often know something I don't about how users actually talk about the product.

Sometimes the changes are not good — passive voice everywhere, jargon that users won't recognise, sentences that bury the action. When that happens, I don't debate style preferences. I point to the style guide and explain which principle applies. That ends most arguments, because now it's not my opinion against theirs — it's a shared standard we both agreed to follow.

---

## What I'm Actually Optimising For

A lot of technical writing focuses on correctness: is the information accurate? That's necessary but not sufficient. I'm also asking: will the user find this when they need it? Will they understand it on first read? Will they know what to do next?

Those questions shape every structural decision — what order topics appear in, whether something gets its own page or a section, how much context to give before the first step. Getting those decisions right is the part of the job I find most interesting, and the part that's hardest to automate.
